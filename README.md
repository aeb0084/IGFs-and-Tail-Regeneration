## Research Highlights: 

1. Brown anole lizards can regenerate their tail during dietary restriction that induces body-mass loss. 
2. Greater body-mass loss was associated with reduced tail regeneration, and this relationship between energetic state and regeneration strengthened as regeneration progressed,  
3. Supplemental IGF2, but not IGF1, alters this relationship such that greater body-mass loss was associated with greater tail regeneration. 

 

## Abstract: 
> "Tissue regeneration is thought to be an energetically demanding process. The insulin and insulin-like signaling (IIS) network regulates physiological processes including growth, cell proliferation, metabolism, reproduction, and wound healing, but its role in linking energetic state to tissue regeneration remains poorly understood. Using the brown anole lizard (Anolis sagrei), we tested the hypotheses that (1) dietary restriction and associated body-mass loss would reduce tail regeneration, and (2) supplemental insulin-like growth factors (IGF1 or IGF2) would improve regeneration. Lizards were assigned to either High or Low (inducing mass loss) diets for four weeks prior to tail autotomy. At the time of tail autotomy, animals received a subcutaneous injection of saline (vehicle control, Low and High Diet groups), IGF1 (Low Diet), or IGF2 (Low Diet) at the time of tail autotomy. Regenerative tail growth and body mass were measured for eight weeks. Greater body-mass loss was increasingly associated with less tail regeneration as regeneration progressed. IGF1 did not alter this relationship. In contrast, IGF2 altered this relationship with greater body-mass loss becoming associated with greater regeneration over time. Importantly, body-mass trajectories were similar among the Low Diet treatment groups, indicating that the IGF2-associated change in the relationship between body-mass loss and regeneration appears to be due to IGF2 modifying how regenerative performance responds to the energetic state under resource limitation. These results support IGF2 as a regulator of tissue regeneration and suggest that insulin-like signaling can modify the relationship between energetic state and regenerative performance without altering body-mass trajectories. "
